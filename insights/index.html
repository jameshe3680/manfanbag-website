Product Development · Practical guide

Why design and manufacturing should begin together

Start with the use case

A bag brief should explain who will use the product, what they carry and where they carry it. A commuter backpack and a weekend bag may share materials, but their organization, access and carrying requirements are different.

Review the construction before the sample

Seams, linings, reinforcement and hardware affect how a design can be made. Reviewing them early creates room to adjust the construction while preserving the visual direction.

Turn feedback into specifications

Record dimensions, materials, colors, branding positions and functional requirements. When a sample changes, update the specification too. The approved sample and written requirements should tell the same story.

Questions for your next brief

What must the bag do well? Which features are essential? Which design details can change if they add unnecessary weight or complexity? Discuss these questions before committing to the first sample.

Design Decisions · Practical guide

Is every new feature worth adding?

Give each feature a job

A pocket, extra zipper or detachable strap should solve a clear user problem. Start with the items the customer carries and the moments when they need access to them.

Consider the whole product

A feature can affect more than its component cost. It may add assembly steps, weight, points of wear or inspection requirements. Evaluate the product as a whole rather than approving each addition in isolation.

Test the trade-off in a sample

Use the sample to review access, comfort and organization with realistic contents. Check whether the feature makes the bag easier to use and whether a simpler construction could achieve the same purpose.

A practical decision rule

Keep features that support the core use case and brand promise. Simplify those that create complexity without a clear user benefit. Record the reasons so later revisions do not undo the decision.

Brand Building · Practical guide

Building trust through product consistency

Define what customers should experience

Brand trust depends on the product matching the promise. Translate that promise into specific expectations for materials, feel, function and finishing rather than relying on the word “quality.”

Make the sample review specific

Check the visible details as well as practical use: zipper access, handle comfort, lining fit, label placement and consistency of finishing. Document the revisions and approval criteria.

Keep production aligned with approval

A sample is useful only when production teams understand what was approved. Maintain current specifications and agree how variations or issues will be reviewed before goods are released.

Use feedback in the next development cycle

Customer feedback can show where the design or construction needs attention. Separate isolated defects from recurring usability concerns, then bring the findings into the next product brief.
